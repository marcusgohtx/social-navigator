# Questions that fit the conversation

This is an editorial guide for the prototype, not a validated psychological intervention. Use it to choose a question that fits the person and moment, rather than trying every category.

| Intention | Possible wording | When it fits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Remember something they shared | "How's the workshop preparation going?" | The friend mentioned a workshop and its current status is unknown |
| Invite a story | "What has been the most fun part of putting it together?" | They seem interested in talking about it; do not assume the whole experience has been fun |
| Understand what matters to them | "What would you like people to take away from it?" | They have already chosen to discuss the project |
| Celebrate on their terms | "What part are you happiest with?" | They described a positive experience; leave room for a mixed answer |
| Offer support without taking over | "Would it help to talk it through, or would you rather have a break from it?" | They have expressed difficulty, rather than the agent inferring distress |
| Share the conversation | "I've been trying something new in class too. Want to hear about it?" | The user has something real to share; never invent their experience |

## Match depth to the invitation

For an ordinary catch-up, start with something answerable without disclosing private information. A question about what has been enjoyable can be as worthwhile as a difficult topic. If a friend gives a short answer or changes the subject, follow their lead. Do not prescribe an escalating sequence of increasingly intimate questions.

One possible follow-up can help the user listen. Make it conditional on an actual response, such as "If she brings up choosing a group size, ask what would make the first session manageable." Do not write both sides of the conversation as if the friend will respond predictably.

## Repair common problems

| Draft | Why to change it | Better option |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Are you anxious that nobody will come?" | Invents a feeling and a feared outcome | "How are you feeling about the workshop?", if they want to discuss it |
| "How is the November move going?" | The month is disputed | "How is packing up the studio going?" |
| "Theo told me he's leaving. Are you next?" | Reveals a confidence and invents a premise | Omit this topic with Maya; a broad paraphrase could still reveal it |
| "Why haven't you followed your dream?" | Assumes failure and pressures the person | Ask about their current interests if they bring the subject up |
| "What is your deepest insecurity?" | Pushes intimacy without an invitation | Start with something they have already chosen to share |

Use a small number of options. The point is to help the user notice and respond to their friend, not to keep them reading a list during the meeting.
